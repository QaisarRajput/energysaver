# What is carbon intensity — and why 2am electricity is cleaner than 6pm

Carbon intensity is a measure of how much CO₂ is emitted per unit of electricity generated. In Great Britain it's measured in grams of CO₂ equivalent per kilowatt-hour (gCO₂/kWh).

A reading of **50 gCO₂/kWh** means the grid is running mostly on wind and solar. A reading of **350 gCO₂/kWh** means gas peakers are covering high demand. The difference matters: running your tumble dryer at 350 gCO₂/kWh produces **7× more carbon** than running it at 50 gCO₂/kWh.

## Why does intensity vary so much?

Britain's grid mixes dozens of generation sources. Renewables (wind, solar, hydro) emit near zero. Gas turbines emit ~400–500 gCO₂/kWh equivalent. Nuclear sits around 12. The moment-to-moment average depends on how much of each is generating right now.

**Demand matters too.** Evening is peak demand — everyone comes home, turns on lights, starts cooking, does laundry. That's when the grid calls on its most expensive, dirtiest "peaker" gas plants.

Overnight (roughly midnight–6am), demand drops by a third. Renewables (especially wind) keep generating. Carbon intensity often drops below 100 gCO₂/kWh in low-wind-plus-solar conditions, and can go under 50 in high-renewable periods.

## The practical upshot for your dryer

A tumble dryer running for 90 minutes draws roughly 2.5 kW — about 3.75 kWh of electricity.

| Time | Typical intensity | CO₂ for one dry cycle |
|------|------------------|----------------------|
| 18:00 (peak) | 280 gCO₂/kWh | **1,050 g CO₂** |
| 02:00 (low-demand) | 95 gCO₂/kWh | **356 g CO₂** |

That's 694 g — nearly 0.7 kg — saved by doing nothing different except when you press start.

## How does the Carbon Intensity API work?

The National Energy System Operator (NESO) publishes a live 48-hour forecast for all of Great Britain, updated every 30 minutes. EnergySaver reads this directly in your browser and combines it with your electricity price to find the sweet spot — the window where both the carbon *and* the cost are low.

The API covers England, Scotland, and Wales. Northern Ireland runs a separate electricity market and is not included.

## The bottom line

Carbon intensity is not a theoretical environmental concept — it's a number that changes by the hour and has a real, measurable impact on the emissions from your home appliances. The good news: the cheapest time to run your flexible appliances and the greenest time often overlap, because both are driven by high renewable supply and low demand.
